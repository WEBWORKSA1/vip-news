How billionaire net worth estimates are made, and why they disagree

Share prices, private holdings, debt and guesswork: what is inside a headline wealth figure.

5 minute read. Written for Vip.News.

When a headline says someone gained or lost billions in a day, no money moved. The figure is an estimate of what that person's assets would be worth if valued at today's prices. Understanding how the estimate is built explains why two respected publishers can print different numbers for the same person on the same day.

Public shares do most of the work

For founders of listed companies, most of the estimate is arithmetic: shares held, multiplied by the share price. Holdings are disclosed in regulatory filings, so this part is reliable. It is also why fortunes swing so sharply. A founder who owns a large stake in one company rises and falls with that single share price.

Private companies are valued by comparison

Private businesses have no daily price. Researchers estimate their value from the most recent funding round, from revenue and profit where known, or by comparing them with similar listed companies. Different researchers pick different comparisons, and the answers diverge.

Debt, pledged shares and cash

Careful estimates subtract known debts and discount shares pledged as collateral for loans. They add an allowance for cash built up from dividends and past share sales, minus taxes and known spending. Much of this is inferred, because nobody is obliged to publish a personal balance sheet.

What is usually left out

  • Assets held through trusts or family members, unless ownership is documented.
  • Art, property and other possessions that are hard to price.
  • Wealth of rulers and heads of state, which is often inseparable from the state itself.

How to read a wealth headline

Treat the figure as a range, not a balance. Look for the date of the valuation, because a number from the annual list and a number from this morning are different things. And remember that paper wealth cannot be spent at face value: selling a large stake moves the price and triggers tax.
