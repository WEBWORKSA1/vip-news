How to verify a viral story about a public figure

Six checks that take two minutes and catch most hoaxes, fake quotes and manipulated images.

5 minute read. Written for Vip.News.

False stories about famous people travel fast because they are surprising and cost nothing to share. Death hoaxes, invented quotes and doctored images appear every week. These checks catch most of them.

1. Find the original source

A screenshot is not a source. Search for the exact claim and trace it to where it first appeared. If the trail ends at an anonymous account or a site you have never heard of, treat the claim as unconfirmed.

2. Look for independent confirmation

Major news about a major figure is reported by several newsrooms within minutes, and wire services such as the Associated Press and Reuters are usually first. One outlet alone is a reason to wait. Ten outlets all citing the same single post are still one source.

3. Check the person's own channels

Verified accounts, official websites and spokespeople confirm or deny quickly when a false story spreads. Check that the account is the real one: impostor accounts copy names and photos.

4. Read the date

Old stories resurface as if new. An article from years ago, shared without its date, can look like breaking news. Open the link and read the publication date before reacting.

5. Be suspicious of perfect quotes

Invented quotes tend to be short, outrageous and attached to a photo. Real quotes have a time, a place and an interviewer. Search the quote in quotation marks. If it appears only on image posts, it is probably fabricated.

6. Examine images and video

Use a reverse image search to see where a picture first appeared. For video, look at hands, teeth, jewellery and background text, where synthetic footage often breaks down, and check whether any newsroom has published the same footage.

When you cannot tell

Do not share it. Waiting an hour costs nothing, and an hour is usually long enough for a real story to be confirmed or a fake one to be debunked.
