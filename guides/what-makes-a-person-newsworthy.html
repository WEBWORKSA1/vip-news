What makes a person newsworthy

The tests editors apply before a name becomes a headline, and where privacy still applies.

4 minute read. Written for Vip.News.

Editors decide many times a day whether something a person did is news. The decision is less arbitrary than it looks. Most newsrooms weigh the same handful of questions.

Power over other people

The strongest test is whether a person's decisions affect others. A head of government, a central banker or the chief executive of a large employer is newsworthy at work almost by definition, because the public has a stake in what they do.

Public role, public conduct

People who seek public attention, such as performers, athletes and candidates, accept scrutiny of the role they chose. That scrutiny has limits. Most editorial codes distinguish between conduct connected to the public role and private matters that are not, such as health or the lives of a person's children.

Scale, novelty and consequence

An event is more likely to be reported when it is large, unexpected or likely to change something. A routine earnings report is a brief; the same report with a surprise resignation is a lead story.

Proximity

Audiences care more about people close to them, geographically or culturally. This is why the same day produces different front pages in different countries, and why any ranking of attention reflects the readers it was drawn from.

The public interest is not whatever interests the public

Curiosity alone does not justify intrusion. Responsible outlets ask whether publishing serves a purpose: exposing wrongdoing, protecting safety, informing a decision the public must make. Gossip can be entertaining and lawful; it is a different product from news, and a careful reader keeps the two apart.
