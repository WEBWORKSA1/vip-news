How to read the news in five minutes without missing what matters

A short routine for staying informed: scan, pick, verify, stop.

5 minute read. Written for Vip.News.

Most people do not have a news problem. They have a time problem. The goal of a five-minute read is not to know everything. It is to know what changed since yesterday and which two stories deserve more of your attention later.

Minute one: scan headlines from more than one publisher

A single outlet shows you one editor's view of the day. Scanning the same story across three publishers shows you what is agreed and what is framing. When three unrelated newsrooms lead with the same event, it matters. When only one does, it may be an exclusive, or it may be a slow day.

Minutes two and three: pick two stories

Choose one story that affects your work or money and one that you are simply curious about. Open the original article for each. Read the first four paragraphs: reputable newsrooms put who, what, when and where at the top, and the background below.

Minute four: check the people

News is made by people making decisions. Ask who decided, who benefits and who is quoted. If a story about a chief executive quotes only the company, you have read a press release. If it quotes a regulator, a rival and an employee, you have read reporting.

Minute five: stop

Close the tab. Research on news habits consistently finds that checking more often does not make people better informed; it makes them more anxious. Set a second time later in the day if you need one, and leave the rest.

Three habits that save the most time

  1. Read at fixed times instead of reacting to alerts.
  2. Prefer original reporting to commentary about reporting.
  3. When a headline makes you angry, read the article before you share it. The headline was written to be clicked; the article was written to be accurate.
